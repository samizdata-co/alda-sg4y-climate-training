# Handout holds up, but needs sharper edges

The SG4Y-CLIMATE workshop handout (`docs/workshop-handout.md`) is **largely accurate and current**: every link in it is live, the three access-to-information deadlines are correct, the Aarhus Convention status is right, and both Council of Europe instruments are real and correctly dated. What it lacks is the detail that decides whether a 20-something's first information request is answered or ignored. It leaves out that a Moldovan request without a postal address can lawfully go unexamined, that a Moldovan or Romanian participant generally cannot file a Turkish request in their own name, and that Romania has a separate, stronger regime for environmental and emissions data. Three items need correcting: the Charter is described as "revised" when it is a new text that the Committee of Ministers adopted in March 2026, Moldova's open data portal has been reorganised, and the Datawrapper entry omits the need for an account. The most useful additions are project context (the handout never explains what SG4Y-CLIMATE or TYCCA stand for, or who funds the project), the Covenant of Mayors status of the three cities (Tekirdağ has a 2024 climate plan, Galați's plan expired in 2020, Chișinău never submitted one), a one-line answer on national youth council rules, and appeal routes for refused requests. Nothing in the handout needs to be removed. Several points cannot be settled from public sources and need confirming with ALDA Moldova before printing: the session time across two time zones, the Council of Europe policy paper, and whether some government portals load from inside each country.

## All three information laws check out, but the handout omits the traps

The deadline table (handout lines 121–125) is correct in all three rows. In **Moldova**, Law 148/2023 Art. 19 requires an answer "no later than 10 days" from registration, extendable by at most 7 days for complex or voluminous requests. The extension only counts if the requester is told in writing, with reasons, within the first 10 days. The article says "days", not "working days", even though the same law says "working days" elsewhere, so a plain reading points to calendar days ([Law 148/2023, FAOLEX](https://faolex.fao.org/docs/pdf/mol221241.pdf)). The law came into force on 8 January 2024 and repealed Law 982/2000 ([CoE IRIS Merlin](https://merlin.obs.coe.int/article/9800)). That is worth saying, because older NGO guides still cite the old law. In **Romania**, Law 544/2001 Art. 7 gives 10 days, or up to 30 if the requester is notified within the first 10, and 5 days to communicate a refusal. The 2024 amendment (Law 122/2024) did not change these deadlines ([Law 544/2001 consolidated](https://ispaim.mapn.ro/webroot/fileslib/upload/files/Legislatie/Legea%20544%20din%202001%20privind%20liberul%20acces%20la%20informatiile%20de%20interes%20public.pdf)). Sources disagree on whether these are working or calendar days: the implementing norms and institutions such as ISU Iași say working days ([ISU Iași](https://isuis.igsu.ro/aplicarea-legii-5442001/informare-legea-5442001-21)), while one municipal guide based on HG 830/2022 says calendar days ([Primăria Videle guide](https://primariavidele.ro/attachments/article/557/Ghidul-Cetateanului-Legea-544_SGD.pdf)). In **Türkiye**, Law 4982 Art. 11 gives **15 working days**, or **30 working days** when the information comes from another unit of the institution or another institution, or when another institution's opinion is needed. The extension must be notified before the 15 days run out ([Law 4982, mevzuat.gov.tr](https://www.mevzuat.gov.tr/mevzuatmetin/1.5.4982.pdf)).

The Aarhus line (127) is also right. Moldova ratified in 1999 and Romania in 2000, and **Türkiye is neither a signatory nor a party** ([UN Treaty Collection](https://treaties.un.org/Pages/ViewDetails.aspx?src=TREATY&mtdsg_no=XXVII-13&chapter=27&clang=_en)). Saying this outright would stop Turkish participants from reading the sentence as covering them. If the facilitator wants a concrete Moldovan example, there is one: in July 2021 the Aarhus Compliance Committee found Moldova in non-compliance in **case ACCC/C/2017/147**, brought by the NGO Eco-TIRAS, mainly over excessive charges for environmental information. The Meeting of the Parties endorsed the finding in October 2021 ([UNECE findings page](https://unece.org/environment/documents/2021/07/working-documents/findings-advance-unedited-25072021-acccc2017147); [Eco-TIRAS](https://eco-tiras.org/219-молдова-не-выполняет-положения-орхусской-конвенции)).

The handout's real weakness is what it leaves out, and three gaps can sink a first request. First, Moldova's law makes the requester's **name and postal address mandatory**, even for email requests, and a request without them "is not examined" (Art. 17(1)). Signatures and reasons are not required, and electronic replies are free (Arts. 16, 22) ([Law 148/2023](https://faolex.fao.org/docs/pdf/mol221241.pdf)). Second, Türkiye's law limits foreigners: those resident in Türkiye can request only information about themselves or their field of activity, on a reciprocity basis (Art. 4). Electronic requests in practice need a **T.C. identity number** ([Law 4982](https://www.mevzuat.gov.tr/mevzuatmetin/1.5.4982.pdf); [SBB Bilgi Edinme](https://www.sbb.gov.tr/bilgi-edinme/)). So the homework line "send them in your own name" (line 99) will not work for a Moldovan or Romanian member of a mixed group writing to Tekirdağ. A Turkish member has to file. Third, Turkish authorities can refuse requests that require "a separate study, research or analysis" (Art. 7), and requests for opinions or advice fall outside the law (Art. 27). Participants should ask for **existing documents**, such as permits, inspection reports, monitoring data and contracts, rather than for analyses.

Romania also has a separate environmental regime that the handout does not mention. **HG 878/2005** (transposing Directive 2003/4/EC) gives one month, or two for complex requests. It requires refusal grounds to be read restrictively against the public interest. It also bars authorities from withholding information on **emissions into the environment** on grounds of commercial confidentiality, personal data or confidentiality of proceedings (Art. 12(4)) ([HG 878/2005, Ministry of Environment](https://www.mmediu.ro/app/webroot/uploads/files/HG_878_2005.pdf)). For a climate workshop, this is the strongest single legal argument available to Romanian participants. Moldova's law sends environmental information to a Government regulation (HG 1467/2016) and states that the public interest prevails when disclosure would help prevent or reveal harm to the environment (Arts. 2(3), 9(3)(f)) ([Law 148/2023](https://faolex.fao.org/docs/pdf/mol221241.pdf)). A 2024 draft would replace that regulation ([particip.gov.md draft](https://particip.gov.md/ro/download_attachment/25273)). Its adoption is unconfirmed.

Appeal routes are missing too, and they differ sharply between the three countries. In Moldova you go **straight to administrative court**, with no prior complaint: within 30 days of a reply, or within one year if there is none. Silence counts as rejection (Arts. 23(5), 25) ([Law 148/2023](https://faolex.fao.org/docs/pdf/mol221241.pdf)). In Romania you can complain to the head of the institution, or go to the tribunal, within 30 days. Court cases are urgent and exempt from stamp duty (Arts. 21–22) ([Law 544/2001](https://ispaim.mapn.ro/webroot/fileslib/upload/files/Legislatie/Legea%20544%20din%202001%20privind%20liberul%20acces%20la%20informatiile%20de%20interes%20public.pdf)). In Türkiye an objection to the Information Access Evaluation Board (Bilgi Edinme Değerlendirme Kurulu) is possible within **15 days**. The Board decides within 30 working days, and the objection pauses the court deadline (Art. 13) ([Law 4982](https://www.mevzuat.gov.tr/mevzuatmetin/1.5.4982.pdf)).

## Every link works, but Moldova's portal has moved and better sources exist

Every URL in the "Useful resources" section (lines 104–117) returned a live page on 3 November 2026. That includes TEMPO Online at its plain-http port-8077 address and the Tekirdağ council-decision search on e-Devlet ([TEMPO Online](http://statistici.insse.ro:8077/tempo-online/); [e-Devlet Tekirdağ](https://www.turkiye.gov.tr/tekirdag-buyuksehir-belediyesi-meclis-karari-sorgulama)). One entry is misleading. **date.gov.md is now a landing page**, the "Portalul Guvernamental de Date", which sends users to a separate catalogue at dataset.gov.md, and that catalogue returned 404 in automated checks. Old date.gov.md/ckan deep links are also broken ([date.gov.md](https://date.gov.md/)). A 404 from outside the country does not prove the catalogue is down for users in Moldova, but the handout should name a reliable backup, the National Bureau of Statistics StatBank ([statbank.statistica.md](https://statbank.statistica.md/PxWeb/pxweb/ro/)). The e-Devlet search may need an e-Devlet login, which would shut out non-Turkish participants. This was not tested. Datawrapper's free plan needs **an account** and adds a "Created with Datawrapper" credit, though it explicitly supports grouping workshop attendees into teams ([Datawrapper pricing](https://www.datawrapper.de/pricing)). Participants should sign up before Saturday, not during the 00:30 tools block.

The bigger opportunity is giving each partner city a source it actually needs. For Galați, **calitateaer.ro** is the national air-quality network portal: more than 100 stations, hourly values, historical reports and a webservice. Galați City Hall links to it directly ([calitateaer.ro](https://www.calitateaer.ro/public/home-page/?__locale=ro)). Galați's own "transparență decizională" page has a long, unguessable URL, so the handout should give the link or tell readers to click through from the homepage ([primariagalati.ro](https://www.primariagalati.ro/)). For Chișinău, the Environment Agency (at **am.gov.md**, not env.gov.md, which does not exist) publishes monthly PDF environmental-quality bulletins through 2026 ([am.gov.md bulletins](https://am.gov.md/ro/rapoarte/buletine-lunare-privind-calitatea-mediului)). The city's "Consultări publice" page currently lists a consultation on environmental and natural-risk studies for the **PUG 2040** general urban plan, a live climate decision participants could respond to ([chisinau.md consultations](https://www.chisinau.md/ro/decision_making_process); [CP-04 announcement](https://www.chisinau.md/ro/act_projects_details/anunt-privind-organizarea-consultarii-publice-cp-04-asupra-studiilor-de-fundamentare-patrimoniu-natural-protectia-mediului-riscuri-naturale-si-riscuri-antropice-si-studiu-istoric-elaborate-in-cadrul-procesului-de-reactualizare-a-planului-urbanistic-general-al-orasului-chisinau-2040/595)). For Tekirdağ, the state meteorological service MGM has a beginner-friendly province climate-statistics page ([MGM Tekirdağ](https://www.mgm.gov.tr/veridegerlendirme/il-ve-ilceler-istatistik.aspx?m=TEKIRDAG)). The statistics office TÜİK's data portal has moved to **veriportali.tuik.gov.tr** ([TÜİK](https://veriportali.tuik.gov.tr/tr/)). The national air-quality site havaizleme.gov.tr timed out from outside Türkiye, so the aggregators AQICN and OpenAQ are safer fallbacks for both Tekirdağ and Chișinău ([AQICN Chișinău](https://aqicn.org/map/chisinau/); [OpenAQ Explorer](https://explore.openaq.org/)). For a pan-European view, the **EEA European Air Quality Index** is at airindex.eea.europa.eu ([EEA AQI](https://airindex.eea.europa.eu/AQI/index.html)), and the **Copernicus Interactive Climate Atlas** shows climate projections without code ([Copernicus Atlas](https://atlas.climate.copernicus.eu/)). If any slide cites Global Forest Watch, note that it became **Global Nature Watch** on 21 September 2026 ([WRI](https://www.wri.org/news/release-global-forest-watch-becomes-global-nature-watch-expanding-cover-more-land-ecosystems)).

The Covenant of Mayors line (109) is accurate but leaves out what makes it useful in a workshop. The three cities sit at very different points. **Chișinău** signed in 2011 with a 2020 commitment and has reported nothing. Its profile still lists former mayor Dorin Chirtoacă, and a UNDP document calls it a suspended signatory ([Chișinău profile](https://eu-mayors.ec.europa.eu/en/signatory/14852); [UNDP](https://sc.undp.md/jobget_doc/5542)). **Galați** signed in 2014, approved a 2020 sustainable energy action plan (SEAP) in October 2016 and last reported monitoring in 2022, but has no 2030 sustainable energy and climate action plan (SECAP) on record ([Galați profile](https://eu-mayors.ec.europa.eu/en/signatory/18130)). **Tekirdağ** signed in January 2023 and approved its SECAP on **10 January 2024**. The plan has a 2019 baseline of 12.05 million tCO2e, targets an 80% cut by 2050, names floods as the main hazard, and includes flood-risk and land-surface-temperature maps ([Tekirdağ profile](https://eu-mayors.ec.europa.eu/en/signatory/31677); [Tekirdağ SECAP PDF, ~20 MB](https://mycovenant.eumayors.eu/storage/web/mc_covenant/documents/31/QOyEAroJ0jU9Fb3q5Z0TNdG0roJbvqG5.pdf)). This three-way contrast is ready-made Exercise 1 material: one city has a plan to hold to account, one has a plan that expired, and one has no plan at all.

## The Charter entry is outdated, and "are authorities obliged?" has a sobering answer

Recommendation **CM/Rec(2024)6** (line 131) is correctly cited. It was adopted on 23 October 2024. Its paragraph 4 asks governments to invite local and regional authorities to apply the measures within their competence, and its appendix calls for "full access to climate-related information and data" ([CM/Rec(2024)6](https://rm.coe.int/cm-rec-2024-6-young-people-and-climate-action/1680b21a0e)). The handout does not call it the "first international instrument" on young people and climate action, and the facilitator should not say so orally without attribution: the label comes from the European youth information network ERYICA, not from the text ([ERYICA](https://eryica.org/news/coe-climate-change-recommendations)). The Charter entry (line 132) needs updating. The Congress of Local and Regional Authorities adopted a **new** European Charter on 29 October 2025, through Resolution 516 (2025) and Recommendation 537 (2025), explicitly replacing the 2003 text. The 2003 text was itself the "Revised" Charter, so calling the 2025 one "revised" invites confusion ([CG(2025)49-14](https://rm.coe.int/cg-2025-49-14finprov-en-european-charter-on-the-participation-of-young/488028e792)). The Committee of Ministers then adopted it as a recommendation to member states on **12 March 2026**, numbered CM/Rec(2026)3 ([CM meeting, 12 March 2026](https://www.coe.int/en/web/cm/-/meeting-of-the-ministers-deputies-on-12-march-2026); [CoE youth news](https://www.coe.int/web/youth/-/adopted-the-european-charter-on-the-participation-of-young-people-in-local-and-regional-life)). That adoption is what makes it a text addressed to the governments of Moldova, Romania and Türkiye. The new Charter covers ground this workshop deals with directly. Paragraph 48 lists "environment and climate change" among the policy areas where youth perspectives must be considered. Paragraph 40 asks authorities to use "online consultations, virtual town halls and interactive decision-making platforms". Paragraphs 31–37 call for independent, youth-led and properly funded youth councils ([CG(2025)49-14](https://rm.coe.int/cg-2025-49-14finprov-en-european-charter-on-the-participation-of-young/488028e792)). The handout's link points to the "finprov" (provisional) version of the Congress report.

The handout asks "are authorities obliged to respond to them?" (line 43) but never answers. The research gives a consistent answer for all three countries: **no youth body is binding**. Moldova's new framework regulation for Local Youth Councils, **OMEC 2057/2025** (published on 30 December 2025, repealing the 2016 order), defines the council as "informal, apolitical" and consultative. Members are aged 14–35 and elected by a general assembly of local young people for two-year terms, and the authority must provide only "minimum resources". Chișinău, as a second-level municipality, would have a Municipal Youth Council made up of delegates, not a single local council. The regulation does not mention climate ([OMEC 2057/2025](https://new.mec.gov.md/sites/default/files/2026-05/152274.pdf)). The order's header is dated 25 October and its signature block 25 November 2025, so "Order 2057/2025" is the safest citation. In Romania, local and county youth councils are **optional consultative bodies** created by council decision, under the general framework of Law 350/2006. They are active only in some places and rarely funded ([Youth Wiki Romania 5.3](https://national-policies.eacea.ec.europa.eu/youthwiki/chapters/romania/53-youth-representation-bodies)). In Türkiye, city councils (kent konseyleri) rest on Art. 76 of Municipal Law 5393, and their youth assemblies are optional: councils "may" create them ([Kent Konseyi Yönetmeliği, Bağcılar copy](https://www.bagcilar.bel.tr/BBImages/Slider/Image/kentkonseyiyonetmeligi.pdf)). Where an obligation does exist, it comes from transparency law. Moldova's Law 239/2008 gives at least **10 working days** to submit recommendations on a draft local decision ([BRI copy of Law 239/2008](https://www.bri.gov.md/sites/default/files/document/attachments/lege239-2008_transparenta%20decizionala.pdf)). Romania's equivalent, Law 52/2003, could not be checked against a primary text.

None of the notes could confirm a standing, climate-active youth council in Chișinău, Galați or Tekirdağ. Galați has held a one-off "local councillors for a day" session with the County Student Council ([Adevărul](https://adevarul.ro/stiri-locale/galati/elevii-alesi-locali-pentru-o-zi-la-galati-cine-a-2172154.html)). Tekirdağ's Kent Konseyi has scheduled youth and children's assembly elections ([Haberler.com](https://www.haberler.com/tekirdag-dan-kisa-kisa-14653930-haberi/)). Exercise 1 asks groups to compare youth mechanisms in these cities, so the facilitator should bring confirmed facts from the partners rather than let groups assume such councils exist.

## SG4Y-CLIMATE is never explained, and the good-practice evidence is thinner than hoped

The handout links to the project (line 14) and refers to "TYCCA working groups" (line 24) without spelling out either acronym or naming the funder. The official programme record fills the gap. SG4Y-CLIMATE ("Smart Governance for Youth Involvement in Climate Action") is project **BSB01316** under **Interreg NEXT Black Sea Basin**, co-financed by the EU. It runs from **4 November 2025 to 3 May 2027** with a budget of €548,716 ([Interreg NEXT BSB](https://blacksea-cbc.net/interreg-next-bsb-2021-2027/projects/bsb01316)). Its four partners are **Istanbul Nişantaşı University** (lead), **ALDA Moldova**, **Galați Municipality** and **Tekirdağ Metropolitan Municipality**. Chișinău City Hall is not a partner. Moldova takes part through ALDA Moldova, ALDA's Chișinău branch ([smartgovernance4youth.org](https://smartgovernance4youth.org/en); [ALDA](https://www.alda-europe.eu/?p=80850)). TYCCA is the **Transnational Youth Council for Climate Action**: 35 young people and 4 mentors in four working groups, on Health Impact, Law and Policy, Innovation and Solutions, and Participation in Decision Making ([TYCCA page](https://smartgovernance4youth.org/en/tycca)). Members were recruited at ages 18–29, with sufficient English ([Tekirdağ TYCCA call](https://www.tekirdag.bel.tr/content/WebSource/file/duyuru/1777972029_tycca_lan_metni.pdf)). The project promises a smart governance methodology and an "AI-enabled digital participation platform" ([Interreg NEXT BSB](https://blacksea-cbc.net/interreg-next-bsb-2021-2027/projects/bsb01316)), which ties naturally into a session on digital tools. Public sources mention neither the platform's URL or status nor a Council of Europe policy paper.

For good practices, the strongest nearby examples are participatory budgets, which show that digital channels can turn into spending decisions. **Sofia Decides**, built on the open-source platform Decidim, drew **11,589 voters** (7,105 online) in its first round and allocated BGN 5.3 million, with "greening" as a project category. Voting is open only to people aged 18 and over ([Interreg Europe](https://www.interregeurope.eu/good-practices/sofia-decides-civic-participation-platform-for-transparent-local-decision-making)). **Com'ON Cluj-Napoca** is a youth participatory budget run with City Hall since 2015. Its Green Mission 2030 strand links youth ideas to the city's climate-neutrality goal, and it won the "Verde pentru Viitor" award in 2025 ([Actual de Cluj](https://actualdecluj.ro/programul-de-bugetare-participativa-pentru-tineri-botezat-comon-cluj-napoca-dublu-premiat-la-gala-tineretului-din-romania-2025/)). Chișinău's civic budget, Bugetul Civic, puts 3 million lei a year into resident projects, with energy efficiency and urban mobility among the eligible themes ([Logos Press](https://logos-pres.md/noutati/locuitorii-capitalei-au-primit-3-milioane-de-lei-pentru-proiectele-lor/)). In Tekirdağ, a 2025 STGM programme had 20 local young people map needs in the Süleymanpaşa district and present proposals at five dialogue meetings with local government ([STGM](https://www.stgm.org.tr/en/young-voices-shaping-their-cities-tekirdag-applications-open-young-voices-participatory-cities)). The research also found an honest counter-example. In 2021, Cluj City Hall refused to host 22 crowdfunded civic air sensors, so residents "adopted" them instead ([Actual de Cluj, 2021](https://actualdecluj.ro/refuzati-de-primarie-civicii-din-cluj-cheama-comunitatea-sa-adopte-cei-22-de-senzorii-de-monitorizare-a-aerului-cumparati-in-urma-unei-campanii-de-strangere-de-fonduri/)). Notably, **no documented 2023–2026 case** turned up in these countries in which a municipality changed a climate decision because of evidence produced by young people. The handout should present that as the gap the workshop sets out to close, not imply it already happens.

## Conclusion

The handout's premise, that the evidence exists and the right to ask for the rest is law, holds up in all three countries. But the research shifts where the practical risk lies. The risk is not wrong deadlines. It is procedural details (a missing postal address, a foreign requester, a request phrased as a question rather than a document) and the fact that in no country are youth bodies more than consultative. The workshop's real leverage therefore comes from combining a legally watertight information request with a deadline-bound consultation channel, such as Moldova's 10 working days under Law 239/2008 or the Chișinău PUG 2040 consultation, rather than relying on youth councils to carry the case. Tekirdağ's 2024 SECAP gives at least one city a published commitment that participants can hold it to.

A mixed group from three countries also changes the homework design. A Turkish request needs a Turkish filer, and Moldovan requests contain home addresses, so the shared request-logging spreadsheet should record the authority, date, registration number and deadline, never personal details.
