## Why these tasks

The training is short (2.5 hours) and online, so whatever participants bring with them makes up most of what they get out of the exercises. The four TYCCA working groups already have campaigns in progress, and together they are preparing a **policy paper for the Council of Europe**, written at campaign level. The tasks aim to:

1. Give the policy paper an evidence base: what youth participation mechanisms already exist, what the Council of Europe has already asked member states to do, and what the numbers say.
2. Push each working group one step forward on its own project, so the workshop feeds the campaign rather than running alongside it.
3. Produce real material (data, charts, gaps) that we can work on together during the session.

The focus is on the two policy-oriented groups, **Law and policies** (project 1) and **Youth participation** (project 3). They get the longest tasks, and their material will anchor the workshop exercises. The garden (project 2) and Eco-Health Pass (project 4) groups get one or two shorter, targeted tasks each.

Everything is designed to take **about 1–1.5 hours per person**. Common tasks are for everyone; group tasks are split among group members.

**Suggested logistics**

- Send the tasks now. If the training is on 7 November, ask for results by **5 November**, so they can be reviewed and used as examples.
- Collect everything in one shared spreadsheet (one tab per task) plus a short intake form. The same spreadsheet becomes the base for the homework and for the evidence annex of the policy paper.
- Working language: English, with sources in Romanian and Turkish welcome.
- If participants can only do one thing, it should be the main task below.

## Main task: One claim, one source, one gap (30–45 minutes, everyone)

Each participant takes **the one sentence their project most wants a decision-maker to believe**, and checks what stands behind it.

1. **The claim.** Write it in one sentence, as it would appear in the policy paper or in a pitch to the mayor.
2. **The best evidence.** Find the strongest source that exists and link it. Rate it: official data, published study, our own survey, or no source.
3. **The gap.** What is missing to prove the claim, and which authority would hold that information? Be as specific as you can: a department, a register, a contract.

Fill one row per person in the shared spreadsheet:

| Claim | Best evidence (link) | Rating | What's missing | Who holds it |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| *A naturalistic garden costs 70% less to maintain than a lawn in Galați.* | *King's College Cambridge meadow study (UK, not Galați)* | *Published study, different context* | *Galați's actual cost of mowing and watering public green spaces* | *Galați City Hall; the municipal maintenance company's contract* |

Starting points for each group (participants can pick a different claim):

- **Law and policies:** "There are no advisory bodies where young people have a say on climate."
- **Youth participation:** "Young people are not consulted on local environmental decisions."
- **Natural garden:** "A 300 m² traditional garden costs about €3,000 a year to maintain."
- **Eco-Health Pass:** "Classrooms above 30°C reduce students' focus by 15–20%."

**Why this task:** all four projects use at least one headline figure or claim without a source. Checking it now avoids that becoming a problem in front of authorities or the Council of Europe. The three steps also map onto the workshop: step 2 is Exercise 1 (finding and assessing data), step 3 is the information requests section, and the whole row is the start of Exercise 2 (the campaign sketch). Together, the rows become the backbone of the policy paper: checked claims, their evidence, and asks for what's missing.

## Other common tasks (everyone)

### Task 0: Intake form (10 minutes)

A short form so the session can be pitched at the right level. Suggested questions:

- Working group, country, city.
- Role (student, PhD, public sector, local/district councillor, NGO, other).
- Have you ever downloaded a dataset and opened it in a spreadsheet? Made a chart? Used a map tool?
- Have you ever sent a request for information to a public authority? Did you get an answer?
- Have you ever taken part in a public consultation, a youth council or a youth assembly?
- What is the one piece of information your group's campaign or the policy paper needs but doesn't have?
- What tools does your group already use (Tableau, Canva, Google Sheets, QGIS, etc.)?

### Task 1: Know your municipality (30 minutes)

Each participant takes their own city (or one of the partner cities: Galați, Chișinău, Tekirdağ) and fills one row in a shared spreadsheet:

| Question | What to record |
|:--|:--|
| Is there a youth council, youth assembly or other youth structure? | Name, link, when it last met (if you can tell), whether it has ever discussed environment or climate |
| Does the municipality announce public consultations on draft decisions? | Link; how many in the last 12 months were about environment, climate, green spaces, water, transport, heat? |
| Does it publish open data? | Link, format (PDF, Excel, CSV), date of the latest update |
| Is there a person or form for information requests? | Name of the role, email or online form |
| Is there a local climate or energy plan (SECAP / Covenant of Mayors, adaptation plan, heat plan)? | Link, year, does it mention young people? |

This becomes the raw material for Exercise 1 and for the "where we stand" section of the policy paper. Comparing three countries side by side is itself a finding: where it's easy, where it's impossible, and why.

Useful starting points:

- Moldova: [particip.gov.md](https://particip.gov.md/) (national consultations), [date.gov.md](https://date.gov.md/) (open data).
- Romania: [data.gov.ro](https://data.gov.ro/) (open data), the municipality's "Monitorul Oficial Local" and "transparență decizională" pages.
- Türkiye: the municipality's council decisions ("meclis kararları"; Tekirdağ's can be searched [through e-Devlet](https://www.turkiye.gov.tr/tekirdag-buyuksehir-belediyesi-meclis-karari-sorgulama)) and city council ("kent konseyi") pages.
- Covenant of Mayors: [eu-mayors.ec.europa.eu](https://eu-mayors.ec.europa.eu/) lists signatory cities and their action plans.

### Optional: Draft one information request

Each group picks the most important gap from the main task (step 3) and drafts (but doesn't send yet) **one** request for that information. We'll review the drafts in the session, then participants send them in their own names. Replies won't arrive before the training, but they can feed the homework and the policy paper.

| Country | Law | Deadline |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Moldova | Law 148/2023 on access to information of public interest | 10 days, extendable by 7 if the requester is notified |
| Romania | Law 544/2001 on free access to information of public interest | 10 days, up to 30 for complex requests |
| Türkiye | Law 4982 on the right to information | 15 working days, up to 30 if other units/institutions are involved |

Moldova and Romania are also parties to the UNECE **Aarhus Convention** on access to environmental information and public participation. Türkiye is not. (Moldova was found in non-compliance by the Convention's Compliance Committee in 2021, following a complaint by the NGO Eco-TIRAS.)

## Background for the policy paper

Two Council of Europe texts are directly on topic, and the policy paper should refer to both. All groups should at least skim their summaries before the session.

- **[Recommendation CM/Rec(2024)6 on young people and climate action](https://rm.coe.int/cm-rec-2024-6-young-people-and-climate-action/1680b21a0e)**, adopted by the Committee of Ministers on 23 October 2024. The Council of Europe describes it as the first international instrument on young people and climate action. It was drafted through the youth sector's co-management system and covers, among other things, including young people in climate decisions, climate education and protecting young environmental defenders.
- **[The European Charter on the Participation of Young People in Local and Regional Life](https://rm.coe.int/cg-2025-49-14finprov-en-european-charter-on-the-participation-of-young/488028e792)**, revised and adopted by the Congress of Local and Regional Authorities on 29 October 2025 (Resolution 516 (2025)). It is not legally binding, but it's the reference text for what municipalities should offer young people, which makes it a good yardstick for Galați, Chișinău and Tekirdağ.

A campaign-level policy paper is strongest when it says: "Our countries have signed up to X. Here is evidence that X isn't happening yet in our cities. Here is what we ask for." The tasks below are built to produce that evidence.

## Group tasks: focus groups

### Project 1, Law and policies: "Youth Voices, Climate Decisions"

**What they're doing:** an advocacy campaign on youth representation, policy influence and institutional accountability, targeting national environment authorities, the European Commission and the Council of Europe.

**What they asked for:** data on EU-level advisory bodies that involve young people, mechanisms for involving young people in climate issues, and statistics.

**A first finding to start from:** the group's working assumption is that there are no advisory councils involving young people. At European level, that isn't quite true: several bodies exist (listed below). The more defensible claim, and probably the more useful one for the campaign, is that **few of them have a climate mandate, few reach down to the local level in our three countries, and few have to respond to what young people say**. The tasks are meant to test that claim with evidence.

**Task L1: Map youth advisory mechanisms (1 hour, split by level).** Build one table of existing mechanisms, from European to local level. Starting list:

| Level | Mechanism | Notes |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Council of Europe | [Advisory Council on Youth](https://www.coe.int/en/web/youth/advisory-council-on-youth) and the Joint Council on Youth (co-management) | Youth organisations decide the youth sector's priorities and budget together with governments. This is the system that drafted CM/Rec(2024)6 |
| Council of Europe | [Congress youth delegates ("Rejuvenating Politics")](https://www.coe.int/en/web/congress/rejuvenating-politics-and-youth-delegates) | One young delegate (18–30) per member state takes part in Congress sessions. Has there been one from Moldova, Romania, Türkiye? |
| EU | [President's Youth Advisory Board](https://youth.europa.eu/strategy/pyab_en) | Set up in June 2025; members come from the national youth councils of EU states (aged 18–28), with observers from candidate countries such as Moldova and Türkiye. No specific climate mandate |
| EU | [Youth Climate and Sustainability Round Tables](https://www.eesc.europa.eu/en/node/100118) (European Economic and Social Committee) | Around ten youth representatives meet the relevant Commissioner, with a follow-up mechanism. The closest to a climate-specific EU youth body |
| EU | EESC Youth Test; Commission Youth Policy Dialogues; [EU Youth Dialogue](https://youth.europa.eu/) | Ways of checking new EU policies for their effect on young people |
| EU | European Climate Pact ambassadors | Individuals, not a council, but a direct climate channel |
| National | Moldova: National Youth Council (CNTM) and the national network of local youth councils; Romania: Romanian Youth Council; Türkiye: Youth Wiki notes efforts to set up a national youth council | Do they have a formal seat in any environment or climate body? |
| Local | Moldova: local youth councils (a new framework regulation was approved by Ministry of Education order 2057/2025); Türkiye: youth assemblies (gençlik meclisleri) under city councils, based on Article 76 of Municipal Law 5393; Romania: local youth councils or consultative structures, where they exist | Do the three partner cities have one? Does it ever discuss climate? |

For each mechanism, record: level, who selects the members, how many young people, does it have a climate or environment mandate, is it consultative or decision-making, and **is the authority obliged to respond** to its proposals. The last column is the core of the campaign's "institutional accountability" pillar.

**Task L2: Find the statistics (45 minutes).** Gather a few numbers the policy paper can use, each with its source:

- **Young people in parliament:** the Inter-Parliamentary Union's [Youth participation in national parliaments](https://www.ipu.org/news/press-releases/2026-04/youth-representation-in-parliament-flatlines-first-time-in-12-years) report (2025 data, published 2026) found that only 2.8% of MPs worldwide are aged 30 or under. Find the figures for Moldova, Romania and Türkiye in the report's annex or the IPU's Parline database.
- **Young people in local councils:** the age breakdown of local councillors elected in the latest local elections, from the electoral bodies (Moldova's Central Electoral Commission, Romania's Permanent Electoral Authority, Türkiye's Supreme Election Council). How many councillors in the three partner cities are under 30 or under 35?
- **Youth participation:** the Flash Eurobarometer on Youth and Democracy (2024, about 26,000 respondents aged 15–30 in the EU, including Romania; a 2026 wave also ran). What share of young people took part in a consultation, a petition or an organisation?
- **Country context:** the EU [Youth Wiki](https://national-policies.eacea.ec.europa.eu/youthwiki) has chapters on youth participation for Romania and Türkiye, and the [EU–Council of Europe Youth Partnership](https://pjp-eu.coe.int/web/youth-partnership/moldova) has material on Moldova.

**Task L3: Read the recommendation (30 minutes).** Skim CM/Rec(2024)6 and list three of its recommendations that the campaign can check in our countries. For each, note one piece of evidence that would show whether it is being implemented. This becomes the skeleton of the policy paper.

### Project 3, Youth participation in decision making: "We speak. They listen. Together we build."

**What they're doing:** a 14-action plan (media, data, policy documents, dialogue), with a three-country survey ready to be visualised and a policy brief for local authorities.

**What they asked for:** data visualisation. The survey is their main data asset, so the tasks focus on getting from a spreadsheet of answers to a few clear charts the policy paper and the campaign can use. We'll critique the drafts live in the session.

**Task Y1: Clean the data (30 minutes).** Export the survey results into one spreadsheet:

- One row per response, one column per question.
- A column for country.
- No names, emails or phone numbers.
- Answer labels written out the same way everywhere ("Agree", not a mix of "agree", "Agree " and "4").

Count the responses per country and write the number down. Every chart should show it, and the paper should present the survey as **what our respondents said**, not as representative of all young people in the three countries.

**Task Y2: Pick three findings and sketch them (30 minutes).** Before opening any tool, write down the three things the survey shows that matter most for the campaign, as sentences (for example, "Most respondents have never been asked for their opinion by their local authority"). Then sketch on paper the chart that would show each one. The [FT Visual Vocabulary](https://github.com/Financial-Times/chart-doctor/tree/main/visual-vocabulary) helps choose a chart type. Photograph the sketches and bring them.

A note on agree/disagree questions: they are usually shown as 100% stacked bars or as diverging stacked bars centred on neutral. Datawrapper has [written about why it prefers stacked bars](https://www.datawrapper.de/blog/divergingbars), which is a useful short read before choosing.

**Task Y3: Build one chart (30 minutes).** Make one of the three charts in [Datawrapper](https://www.datawrapper.de/) (free, no coding, good for charts embedded in web pages and PDFs) or in the group's current tool. Note that Tableau Online is a paid product, and Tableau Public is free but publishes the underlying data openly, which may matter for survey data. Bring the link to the session.

**Task Y4 (optional): Find a comparison.** Find one question in the Flash Eurobarometer on Youth and Democracy (see L2) that is close to one of yours. Being able to say "in our survey X%, EU-wide Y%" makes a chart much stronger. Coordinate with the Law and policies group, which is looking at the same sources.

## Group tasks: other groups

### Project 2, Natural garden pilot in Galați

**What they asked for:** data on water consumption and how much water different plants use.

**Task I1: Estimate water needs (45 minutes).** Plant water use is usually estimated with a simple formula: **water need = reference evapotranspiration (ET₀) × plant factor − effective rainfall**.

- *ET₀ and rainfall for Galați:* [Open-Meteo's historical weather API](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs/historical-weather-api) provides daily FAO reference evapotranspiration (`et0_fao_evapotranspiration`) and precipitation for any coordinates, free and with no account. Download last summer (June–August) for Galați.
- *Plant factors:* the WUCOLS classification from the University of California puts plants in four groups relative to a cool-season lawn. Typical values are about 0.8 for a lawn (high), 0.4–0.6 for moderate, 0.1–0.3 for low and under 0.1 for very low water-use plants. WUCOLS was developed for California, so treat the values as a starting point; a plant's category can change with climate.
- *Do the maths:* for a 300 m² plot, how many litres would a lawn need last summer compared with a low-water planting? Multiply by the local water tariff (published by the Galați water utility) to get a cost.

This gives the pilot a predicted baseline to compare its real meter readings against, and checks the press release's cost figures (€3,000 a year, 70% savings), which currently have no source.

**Task I2 (optional): Local consumption data.** Look up water consumption for Galați in the Romanian statistics institute's [TEMPO database](http://statistici.insse.ro:8077/tempo-online/) (search for drinking water distributed to consumers by locality). This puts the garden's water use in the context of the city's.

A useful model for the monitoring itself is the King's College Cambridge study comparing a wildflower meadow with a lawn ([Ecological Solutions and Evidence, 2023](https://eprints.glos.ac.uk/15393/)), which published its [monitoring data on Dryad](https://datadryad.org/dataset/doi:10.5061/dryad.kd51c5bbb).

### Project 4, Eco-Health Pass

**What they asked for:** something interesting to put in the app, so it isn't empty when young people first open it.

**Task H1: Seed the map with open data (45 minutes).** Pull existing data for Chișinău, Galați and Tekirdağ and load it as starting layers:

- *Drinking water points, parks, bus stops, trees:* in [overpass turbo](https://overpass-turbo.eu/), wizard queries such as `amenity=drinking_water in Chișinău`, `leisure=park in Galați` or `highway=bus_stop in Tekirdağ` return what OpenStreetMap already knows. Export as GeoJSON. Bus stops are natural candidates for "Cool Stops"; mapped refill points are a starting layer for "Refill". Anything the group adds can be contributed back to OpenStreetMap.
- *Live temperature from citizen sensors:* the [Sensor.Community](https://sensor.community/) network has volunteer-run sensors that measure temperature and humidity as well as air quality, including about a dozen in Chișinău. Their data is open. A layer of these sensors would show users that citizen measurements already exist, and give the app's self-reported temperatures something to compare with.
- *Heat over time:* from [Open-Meteo](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs/historical-weather-api), count the days above 30°C each summer in each city over the past 20 years. A "hot days per year" chart in the app is a simple, verifiable hook for the Cool pillar.

**Task H2 (optional): Seed challenges.** Use the layers above to write three or four starter missions, for example "Check the temperature at the bus stop with no shade nearest your school" or "Find a refill point that's on the map but doesn't work". Real places make the demo much more convincing to municipalities.

The group's own source check for the health figures in its infographics remains important before anything is published. Two pointers: studies of heat and learning (Park, Goodman and others, *American Economic Journal: Economic Policy*, 2020) don't support a "15–20% drop in focus above 30°C", and studies of PET bottles in the heat mostly measure chemicals such as antimony leaching out, not microplastics.

Since participants already work in four TYCCA groups and the focus is on projects 1 and 3, it may work better to run Exercise 1 around the L1 table (all groups contribute their country's mechanisms), and Exercise 2 around the project 3 charts and the policy paper's asks.

## Notes for the organisers

- The working group documents include unsourced figures (garden costs, health effects). The main task and the pointers above are meant to fix that before anything is published under the project's name.
- The survey results and self-reported temperatures are fine as campaign signals but should be labelled as such in the policy paper and in anything sent to municipalities.
- The Eco-Health Pass app collects photos, GPS check-ins and survey answers. A short privacy notice (GDPR in Romania, Moldova's personal data protection law, Türkiye's KVKK, Law 6698) will make municipalities more comfortable using the data.
- Information requests should be sent by participants in their own names, after the group agrees on the wording, and logged in the shared spreadsheet so replies aren't lost.
- The legal deadlines and some institutional details (for example the Committee of Ministers' follow-up on the revised Charter) come from secondary sources and should be checked against official texts before they go into the policy paper.
